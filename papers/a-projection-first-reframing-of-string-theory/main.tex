\begin{abstract}
String theory is widely regarded as the most successful ultraviolet framework for quantum
gravity, yet its conceptual status remains unsettled. Are strings and branes fundamental
constituents of nature, or are they effective devices whose success reflects a deeper structural
constraint? This paper develops a projection-first reframing of string theory in which the
distinguishing features of the subject---extended excitations, closed-string universality of
gravity, boundary-localized gauge sectors, holography, modular finiteness, and the apparent
``landscape''---are interpreted as consequences of finite admissibility of physical description
rather than as evidence for a literal string ontology.

The reframing adopts a conservative stance. No new dynamical principles are introduced, no
modification of worldsheet or target-space equations is proposed, and no claims are made about
the ultimate microscopic degrees of freedom beyond effective description. Instead, the paper
identifies which structural conditions any stable, local, predictive description must satisfy and
shows how string-theoretic constructions can be organized as admissible encodings optimized for
maintaining those conditions at high energy. In this view, quantization is not a universal
procedure but a representational strategy valid only within admissible regimes; the special role
of closed-string modes reflects the global bookkeeping required to maintain consistency of
projected descriptions; D-branes are interpreted as admissibility boundaries that localize certain
constraint-carrying modes; and holography is understood as the survival of description on
lower-dimensional loci when bulk encoding becomes inadmissible. The ``landscape'' is
reinterpreted as a catalogue of mathematically consistent encodings, most of which fail
admissibility and therefore do not correspond to physically realizable descriptions.

The goal is explanatory rather than competitive: to provide a vocabulary in which string
theory's characteristic successes and limitations are natural consequences of projection and
finite admissibility, and to clarify how string-theoretic language interfaces with other
projection-based encodings, including semiclassical gravity, effective field theory, and the
constructive quantum-gravity results established elsewhere in the corpus.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
String theory occupies a unique position in modern theoretical physics. It provides a framework
in which ultraviolet divergences of point-particle quantum field theory are softened, gravity
appears unavoidably through closed-string excitations, gauge sectors arise naturally on
boundaries, and holographic correspondences offer remarkably powerful dual descriptions. At
the same time, the theory is often discussed in terms of an enormous space of backgrounds and
compactifications, and its foundational interpretation is frequently framed as ontological: the
world is made of strings (and branes), and spacetime is a dynamical arena populated by their
quantized excitations.

This paper develops a different perspective. The starting point is the observation that many
central structures of string theory are best understood as \emph{consistency devices} rather than
as direct evidence for a particular microscopic ontology. Modular invariance, BRST nilpotency,
anomaly cancellation, and the requirement of conformal invariance do not behave like optional
model-building choices. They behave like \emph{admissibility conditions}: criteria that decide
whether an effective description can be maintained at all. Likewise, the extended nature of
string excitations appears less as an arbitrary postulate and more as a mechanism that prevents
the failure of description associated with pointlike ultraviolet localization. These features
suggest a broader organizing principle: that stable physical description is possible only through
projection to an effective encoding, and that such projection is valid only within finite
admissibility margins.

The projection-first viewpoint has been developed in the companion reframing papers of this
series. In that framework, \emph{projection} refers to the structural necessity of identifying
microscopic distinctions in order to obtain a stable, local, predictive effective description.
\emph{Admissibility} refers to the boundedness, spectral separation, and stability margins that
must hold for such a projection to remain controlled. When admissibility holds, effective
descriptions may be locally invertible and may admit familiar structures such as Hilbert spaces,
operator algebras, and unitary evolution. When admissibility fails, effective description becomes
noninvertible, and phenomena such as irreversibility, entropy bounds, horizon behavior, and
breakdown of global reconstruction arise as structural necessities rather than as signals of
missing dynamics.

Applying this viewpoint to string theory yields a reframing with three immediate consequences.

\paragraph{Strings as capacity-optimal encodings.}
If projection is finite and must remain stable under high-frequency disturbance, then pointlike
encodings generically exceed admissibility: they require arbitrarily fine distinctions and lead to
ultraviolet sensitivity. Extended encodings distribute coherence and reduce the strain on
projection. In this sense, the appearance of one-dimensional extended excitations is not an
ontological postulate but a capacity-optimal response: strings are the minimal extended
structures that preserve local predictivity while suppressing ultraviolet instability. Their
``vibrational spectrum'' is then interpreted as the spectral decomposition of stable eigenmodes
of an admissible extended encoding, rather than as literal microscopic oscillations of a material
object.

\paragraph{Closed strings and gravity as global bookkeeping.}
The closed-string sector plays a special role in string theory: it is unavoidable, universal, and
couples to all other sectors. In a projection-first language, this is the signature of bookkeeping
rather than substance. Maintaining mutual compatibility of local projected descriptions across
regions requires a global consistency structure. Gravity is the effective shadow of this
consistency requirement. Closed-string modes are therefore interpreted as globally coherent
bookkeeping modes that cannot be localized without destroying the consistency of the projected
description, explaining both their universality and why gravity is not naturally confined to
lower-dimensional sectors.

\paragraph{Branes as admissibility boundaries and the landscape as a catalogue.}
D-branes are usually presented as physical objects on which open strings end. Projection-first
reasoning instead emphasizes their structural role: they implement boundary conditions that
localize certain modes and restrict which encodings remain admissible. Brane tension is then
naturally interpreted as a capacity cost: the stability budget expended per unit boundary volume
to enforce localization constraints and maintain consistent projection. On cosmological and
compactification scales, the same logic reframes the ``landscape.'' The landscape is not a space
of physically realized vacua among which nature must choose, but a catalogue of mathematically
consistent encodings, most of which fail admissibility once boundedness, spectral separation,
and stability margins are imposed. Predictivity is restored not by anthropic selection but by
structural elimination of non-admissible descriptions.

The remainder of this paper develops these points in a deliberately conservative manner. We do
not attempt to rederive the Polyakov action, compute string amplitudes, or replace standard
duality arguments. Instead, we aim to clarify why string theory repeatedly converges on the
same motifs---minimal length, holography, modular finiteness, brane-localized gauge sectors,
and strong constraints on backgrounds---and to show that these motifs are natural consequences
of projection with finite admissibility. In doing so, we provide a translation dictionary between
standard string-theoretic language and projection-first terminology, and we delineate the
domain in which string theory functions as a powerful admissible encoding of high-energy
physics.

\paragraph{Relation to the rest of the MTT corpus.}
No familiarity with Modal Triplet Theory is required to follow the arguments here. However, the
projection-first viewpoint is informed by results established in the MTT corpus, where bounded
projectors, spectral gaps, and admissible slabs can be made explicit and where standard quantum
and gravitational structures arise as regime-limited shadows of coherent-sector projection. The
present paper should therefore be read as a reframing of string theory's conceptual status, not
as a replacement for its technical machinery.

\section{Projection, Admissibility, and Descriptive Capacity}

This section introduces the minimal projection-first concepts needed to reinterpret string
theory. The goal is not to replace standard string-theoretic language, but to clarify which
structural constraints that language is already enforcing implicitly.

\subsection{Projection as a structural necessity}

Any predictive physical description requires a reduction of microscopic distinctions. In
projection-first language, this reduction is called \emph{projection}: a many-to-one mapping
from a high-dimensional configuration space to a lower-dimensional effective description.
Projection is not an approximation or a choice; it is a structural requirement for locality,
stability, and predictivity.

Projection is admissible only if three conditions hold simultaneously:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Boundedness}: small disturbances in the underlying configuration do not produce
  unbounded changes in the projected description.
  \item \textbf{Spectral separation}: the operators governing admissible degrees of freedom
  possess gaps that prevent uncontrolled leakage into discarded directions.
  \item \textbf{Stability}: the projected description is dynamically self-consistent over the
  timescales on which it is intended to apply.
\end{enumerate}
When these conditions are satisfied, effective notions such as fields, particles, and unitary
time evolution may exist. When they fail, effective description itself breaks down rather than
becoming merely inaccurate.

\subsection{Admissibility and the notion of capacity}

Admissibility is not unlimited. Every projection-based description has a finite \emph{capacity}:
a bound on how much independent structure, distinction, or coherence it can sustain before
boundedness or invertibility fails. Capacity is not a conserved quantity, not a field, and not
an observable. It is a structural limit, analogous to a radius of convergence or a condition
number, that determines whether a description exists at all.

Exceeding capacity does not produce a new phase of the same description. It produces a loss of
description. Phenomena such as ultraviolet divergences, horizon formation, entropy bounds, and
noninvertible measurement outcomes are all signatures of capacity exhaustion rather than
indications of missing microscopic dynamics.

\subsection{Geometry as bookkeeping}

Within a projection-first framework, spacetime geometry does not represent an additional
dynamical substance. Instead, it functions as bookkeeping: it synchronizes local projected
descriptions, enforces causal compatibility, and tracks how admissibility constraints vary
across regions. Gravity is the effective shadow of this bookkeeping role. Its universality and
its resistance to localization follow from the requirement that bookkeeping remain coherent
globally.

This viewpoint will be essential in understanding why string theory inevitably contains a
closed-string sector and why that sector cannot be consistently removed.

\section{Why Strings Appear and Why They Vibrate}

We now address the central structural question: why do admissible high-energy descriptions take
the form of extended, one-dimensional objects with discrete excitation spectra?

\subsection{Failure of pointlike encodings}

Point-particle quantum field theory assumes that arbitrarily localized excitations remain
admissible at arbitrarily high energy. From a projection-first perspective, this assumption
fails generically. Pointlike localization requires access to arbitrarily high-frequency modes,
which rapidly exhaust capacity by destabilizing projection and destroying boundedness. The
resulting ultraviolet pathologies are not technical accidents; they signal loss of admissible
description.

\subsection{Extended encodings as capacity-optimal}

Extended encodings distribute coherence over finite regions and thereby reduce the strain on
projection. Among extended objects, one-dimensional encodings occupy a special position: they
are the minimal structures that suppress ultraviolet localization while preserving locality
and controllability. Higher-dimensional extended objects consume capacity too rapidly, while
pointlike encodings consume it immediately.

From this perspective, strings arise not as fundamental constituents but as
\emph{capacity-optimal encodings}: the simplest structures capable of sustaining admissible
projection at high frequency.

\subsection{Vibration as spectral decomposition}

Once an extended encoding is selected, its stable configurations are determined by the spectral
properties of the operators governing admissible disturbances. Discrete excitation modes arise
as normal modes of the constrained system. What string theory describes as ``vibrations'' are
therefore not literal oscillations of a material filament, but the spectral decomposition of
stable eigenmodes of an admissible extended encoding.

Mass spectra, spin assignments, and degeneracies are fixed by symmetry and boundary conditions,
exactly as in any constrained spectral problem. Quantization of these modes is not an added
postulate but a consequence of admissibility: only discrete eigenmodes remain stable under
projection.

\subsection{Background dependence revisited}

Because admissibility depends on boundedness and spectral separation, the allowed excitation
modes depend sensitively on background structure. Changing the background geometry alters which
encodings remain admissible and therefore which spectra exist at all. This explains why string
theory is highly background-sensitive and why different backgrounds are not smoothly related by
small perturbations within a single Hilbert space.

In projection-first terms, a ``background'' specifies the admissibility conditions of the
encoding. Changing it changes the rules of description, not merely the values of dynamical
fields.

\section{Closed Strings as Global Bookkeeping Modes}

One of the most robust features of string theory is the inevitability of closed-string
excitations. Even in constructions designed to isolate gauge or matter sectors, the closed
sector reappears as a consistency requirement. From a projection-first perspective, this
behavior is not accidental and does not rely on the specific dynamics of the worldsheet.
It reflects the structural role of closed strings as global bookkeeping modes.

\subsection{Universality and nonlocalizability}

Closed-string modes propagate throughout the bulk and couple universally to all other
sectors. Attempts to confine or remove them lead to inconsistencies, anomalies, or loss of
background coherence. In standard string-theoretic language this universality is often
explained by the appearance of a massless spin-two excitation and its required coupling to
energy--momentum.

Projection-first reasoning inverts this explanation. Maintaining compatibility among local
projected descriptions across regions requires a globally coherent structure. Such a
structure cannot be localized without destroying the mutual consistency of projection.
Closed-string modes are therefore interpreted as the admissible carriers of this global
consistency. Their nonlocalizability is not a dynamical accident but a structural necessity:
localization would correspond to breaking the bookkeeping required to synchronize effective
descriptions.

\subsection{Gravity as consistency enforcement}

In this reframing, gravity is not an additional interaction layered on top of other fields.
It is the effective shadow of the global bookkeeping required to preserve admissibility.
Geometric degrees of freedom track how admissibility conditions vary across regions and
ensure that local projected descriptions remain mutually compatible.

This explains several familiar facts in a unified way. Gravity couples universally because
bookkeeping must apply to all sectors. Gravitational backreaction is unavoidable because
changes in local constraint load must be reflected in the global consistency structure.
Finally, gravity resists confinement because bookkeeping cannot be restricted to a subset of
degrees of freedom without violating global admissibility.

\subsection{Relation to background equations}

Worldsheet conformal invariance and the resulting target-space equations are often interpreted
as dynamical field equations for geometry. In projection-first terms, these conditions play a
different role: they enforce the admissibility of the background encoding. Backgrounds that
fail these conditions do not represent alternative dynamical solutions; they represent
encodings that cannot sustain stable projection.

From this standpoint, the closed-string sector enforces the admissibility of the entire
encoding. Its equations of motion are constraints on description rather than microscopic
laws governing fundamental constituents.

\section{D-Branes as Admissibility Boundaries}

D-branes are conventionally introduced as physical hypersurfaces on which open strings end.
They carry tension, support gauge fields, and gravitate. Projection-first reasoning instead
emphasizes their structural role: D-branes implement admissibility boundaries where the
character of projection changes.

\subsection{Boundary conditions and projection constraints}

In string theory, open strings satisfy Dirichlet boundary conditions that confine their
endpoints to D-branes. These boundary conditions restrict which modes remain admissible and
which extensions of coherence are forbidden. In projection-first language, this corresponds
to a modification of projection constraints: certain directions in configuration space are
no longer admissible beyond the boundary.

Thus a D-brane is not fundamentally an object embedded in spacetime, but a region where
admissibility conditions enforce localization of specific encodings. Open-string modes are
those modes whose stability requires such localization.

\subsection{Gauge sectors as boundary-stabilized encodings}

Gauge fields and matter degrees of freedom arise naturally from open-string modes localized
on D-branes. Projection-first reasoning explains this localization structurally. Gauge
degrees of freedom carry constraints that require stable local distinguishability. In many
settings, such stability cannot be maintained globally without exhausting capacity. Boundary
localization provides a way to preserve admissibility by restricting the domain over which
these constraints must be enforced.

The disappearance of gauge sectors when branes are removed is then immediate: the boundary
that stabilized the encoding no longer exists, and the corresponding degrees of freedom
cease to be admissible.

\subsection{Brane tension as capacity cost}

D-brane tension is usually interpreted as an energy density associated with a physical object.
Projection-first reasoning offers a complementary interpretation. Enforcing an admissibility
boundary consumes capacity: it restricts the space of admissible encodings and reduces the
degrees of freedom available to maintain global coherence. The greater the restriction, the
higher the capacity cost.

Brane tension is therefore interpreted as the capacity expenditure per unit boundary volume
required to maintain localization constraints. This interpretation explains several standard
features without additional assumptions. High-tension branes are rigid because relaxing their
constraints would exceed admissibility. Branes gravitate because the global bookkeeping
structure must adjust to account for lost capacity. Brane backreaction reflects the cost of
maintaining the boundary within a coherent encoding.

\subsection{Stacks, intersections, and enhanced symmetry}

Stacks of coincident branes impose stronger localization constraints and therefore consume
greater capacity. The resulting enhancement of gauge symmetry reflects the increased
constraint density stabilized on the boundary. Intersections of branes correspond to regions
where multiple admissibility boundaries overlap, producing further localized encodings with
distinct constraint structures.

These phenomena are naturally organized in projection-first language as variations in how
admissibility boundaries partition configuration space, rather than as independent dynamical
objects interacting in spacetime.

\section{Spectral Gaps, Massless Modes, and Moduli}

A recurring feature of string constructions is the coexistence of generically gapped spectra
with special gapless sectors. Massless modes appear on branes, at critical points, or under
enhanced symmetry, while generic bulk configurations exhibit spectral gaps. This section
explains why this pattern is expected from projection-first admissibility rather than a result
of tuning.

\subsection{Gaps as stability barriers}

Spectral gaps are not optional details of an encoding. They function as stability barriers
that prevent uncontrolled leakage of coherence into discarded directions. When a gap exists,
projection remains bounded and admissible. When a gap closes, arbitrarily small disturbances
can destabilize the encoding, leading to loss of invertibility and breakdown of effective
description.

For elliptic operators on compact internal sectors, gapped spectra are structurally stable:
small perturbations of background data do not close gaps. Gapless configurations therefore
form special loci rather than generic interiors. From a projection-first standpoint, gapped
spectra characterize admissible interior regions, while gap closure signals proximity to
admissibility boundaries.

\subsection{Boundary-protected gapless modes}

Massless modes localized on D-branes are a familiar feature of string theory. Projection-first
reasoning explains their localization and protection. At admissibility boundaries, certain
constraints must be enforced exactly to maintain local consistency. This enforcement can
force the closure of specific spectral gaps, producing gapless modes that remain stable only
because they are confined to the boundary.

Thus massless open-string modes are not generic bulk features. They are boundary-stabilized
encodings whose gaplessness is protected by the admissibility constraints defining the
boundary itself. Away from such boundaries, gaplessness is unstable and therefore rare.

\subsection{Moduli as marginal admissibility directions}

Moduli fields correspond to continuous deformations of background data that preserve
worldsheet consistency. In projection-first language, moduli parametrize directions along
which admissibility is marginal: the encoding remains admissible, but stability margins are
reduced. Small perturbations can easily drive the system toward boundary behavior.

This explains why moduli require stabilization. Stabilization is not an aesthetic preference
but a restoration of admissibility margins. Unstabilized moduli signal encodings balanced
precisely at the edge of capacity, where predictive description becomes fragile.

\section{Holography and the Survival of Description}

Holography is one of the most striking features of string theory. Dual descriptions relate
bulk gravitational dynamics to lower-dimensional boundary theories with no gravity. From a
projection-first viewpoint, holography is not a mysterious equivalence of ontologies but a
structural response to capacity exhaustion.

\subsection{Bulk capacity exhaustion}

As the complexity or scale of a bulk region increases, the capacity required to maintain a
local, invertible description grows. When this requirement exceeds available capacity, bulk
projection ceases to be admissible. Description does not vanish; it reorganizes. The surviving
encoding is forced onto regions where admissibility can still be maintained, typically
lower-dimensional boundaries.

This mechanism is independent of specific dynamics. It follows directly from the finite
capacity of projection and the requirement of stability.

\subsection{AdS/CFT reinterpreted}

In this light, AdS/CFT is interpreted as a relation between two admissible encodings of the
same constrained coherence region. The bulk gravitational description and the boundary
conformal field theory are not dual ontologies but alternative charts valid in complementary
regimes. Neither description is globally valid on its own.

The boundary theory persists when bulk description fails because boundary encodings require
less capacity. Holography thus reflects the survival of description under capacity constraints
rather than a fundamental equivalence between bulk and boundary degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Why holography is ubiquitous}

Holographic behavior appears across string theory, black-hole physics, and condensed-matter
systems because it is a generic response to finite capacity. Whenever bulk description becomes
inadmissible, lower-dimensional encodings dominate. Holography is therefore expected wherever
projection is finite, not a peculiarity of special backgrounds.

\section{The Landscape as an Admissibility Catalogue}

The ``string landscape'' is often presented as a vast space of physically realized vacua,
raising questions of predictivity and selection. Projection-first reasoning reframes this
problem by distinguishing mathematical consistency from physical admissibility.

\subsection{Consistency versus admissibility}

Worldsheet consistency conditions determine whether a background defines a mathematically
consistent encoding. They do not guarantee that the encoding remains stable, bounded, or
invertible under projection. Most points in the landscape satisfy consistency but fail
admissibility once stability margins and capacity constraints are imposed.

From a projection-first standpoint, such points do not represent alternative physical worlds.
They represent encodings that cannot sustain a stable description.

\subsection{Admissibility filtering}

Applying admissibility criteria---bounded projection, spectral separation, stability under
disturbance, and finite capacity---dramatically reduces the effective landscape. Encodings
with runaway moduli, vanishing gaps, or excessive constraint load are eliminated prior to any
dynamical or anthropic considerations.

What remains is not a combinatorial explosion but a structured set of coherent basins, often
connected by smooth deformations. Variation persists, but it is constrained and organized
rather than arbitrary.

\subsection{Predictivity without anthropic selection}

This reframing restores limited predictivity without invoking observer-based selection. The
elimination of non-admissible encodings is structural, not environmental. The ``choice'' of
background is replaced by the requirement that description remain admissible.

Anthropic reasoning may still be relevant within admissible families, but it is no longer
required to explain the gross multiplicity of mathematically consistent vacua.

\section{Relation to Dark Matter and Dark Energy}

The projection-first reframing of string theory naturally interfaces with cosmological
phenomena that have resisted particle-based explanations, notably dark matter and dark
energy. Although a detailed treatment is deferred to a dedicated reframing paper, it is
important to clarify how the present interpretation situates these phenomena structurally.

\subsection{Dark matter as constraint load without particle encoding}

In string-theoretic constructions, gravitational effects often persist even when no
corresponding particle excitations are present in the low-energy spectrum. Projection-first
reasoning interprets this behavior as a generic admissibility feature. Gravity, understood as
global bookkeeping, remains operative so long as geometric coherence can be maintained. By
contrast, particle-level encodings require additional capacity to sustain localized,
distinguishable excitations.

Dark matter phenomenology arises when the gravitational bookkeeping structure remains
admissible while particle-level encodings fail. In such regimes, curvature responds to
constraint load that cannot be represented by admissible particle states. This produces
gravitational effects without new particle degrees of freedom. From this standpoint, the
absence of direct detection is not surprising: the relevant encoding does not exist.

\subsection{Dark energy as global capacity exhaustion}

At cosmological scales, maintaining global invertibility and coherence of projection becomes
increasingly difficult. When capacity is exhausted globally, the only admissible response is a
reorganization of the bookkeeping structure itself. Accelerated expansion is interpreted as
geometry relaxing to preserve admissibility by diluting correlations and reducing constraint
density.

In this reframing, dark energy is not vacuum energy in the usual sense, nor a new dynamical
field. It is the large-scale manifestation of finite capacity. String theory, with its
emphasis on holography and entropy bounds, already encodes many aspects of this behavior,
though it does not name it explicitly.

\section{Limits of the Encoding}

A projection-first reframing clarifies not only why string theory succeeds where it does, but
also where its explanatory scope should be expected to end.

\subsection{What string theory should not be asked to explain}

String theory is often burdened with expectations that exceed its natural role as an
admissible encoding. It should not be expected to provide:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a unique global vacuum selected by dynamics alone;
  \item a globally valid quantization of geometry across all regimes;
  \item a resolution of singularities without loss of description;
  \item a particle-based account of all gravitational phenomena.
\end{itemize}
These demands correspond to attempts to extend an encoding beyond its admissibility domain.
Failure in such extensions does not signal incompleteness of the theory, but misuse of its
descriptive regime.

\subsection{Breakdown as structural, not dynamical}

When string-theoretic descriptions fail---for example, near singularities, strong curvature,
or cosmological boundaries---the failure is structural. Projection becomes noninvertible,
spectral gaps close, or stability margins vanish. No modification of local dynamics can repair
this loss. The correct response is not to introduce additional degrees of freedom, but to
recognize that description itself has reached its limit.

This perspective aligns string theory with other projection-based encodings, including
semiclassical gravity and effective field theory. Each is powerful within its admissible
domain and breaks down sharply at its boundaries.

\section{Summary and Outlook}

String theory has long been regarded as a candidate fundamental theory of nature. The
projection-first reframing developed here proposes a different interpretation: string theory
is best understood as a capacity-optimal encoding that preserves admissible description at
high energy. Its defining features---extended excitations, discrete spectra, unavoidable
gravity, boundary-localized gauge sectors, holography, and a constrained landscape---are not
independent postulates but structural responses to finite admissibility.

From this perspective, strings do not exist because the universe is made of strings. They
exist because pointlike encodings fail under finite capacity. Closed-string modes are global
bookkeeping structures required to maintain consistency of projection, while open strings and
D-branes arise at admissibility boundaries where certain encodings must be localized. Brane
tension measures the capacity cost of enforcing such boundaries. Holography reflects the
survival of description on lower-dimensional loci when bulk encoding becomes inadmissible.
The landscape is reinterpreted as a catalogue of mathematically consistent encodings, most of
which fail admissibility before physics begins.

This reframing does not replace string theory's technical machinery, nor does it diminish its
calculational successes. Instead, it clarifies why those successes are possible and why they
come with sharp limitations. In doing so, it situates string theory within a broader
projection-first framework that also encompasses quantum mechanics, gravity, dark sectors,
and limits of computation.

Future work will develop these connections further. In particular, a projection-first
reframing of dark matter and dark energy will make explicit the role of capacity and
admissibility at cosmological scales, and further bridge papers will clarify the relation
between string-theoretic encodings and other admissible descriptions. The hope is that by
making the structural logic explicit, long-standing conceptual tensions can be resolved
without sacrificing the empirical and mathematical strengths of existing frameworks.
