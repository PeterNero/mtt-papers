Coherent-Sector Reduction in Modal Triplet Theory:\\[0.25em]
\large Feshbach Control, Projector Stability, and Local Universality}
\author{Peter Nero}
\date{Version 2, July 2026}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
A coherent-sector truncation is controlled only after the retained
subspace, the full closed operator, its block domains, the spectral
parameter, the eliminated-sector resolvent, and the comparison norm have
all been specified.  A spectral gap by itself is not a complete
truncation theorem.

This paper gives a domain-explicit coherent-sector reduction for a
self-adjoint block operator on
\(\cH=\cH_P\oplus\cH_Q\).  It first resolves a basic ambiguity: if \(P\)
is the exact spectral projector of the same operator \(T\), then \(P\)
reduces \(T\), the off-diagonal blocks vanish, and the truncation is exact.
A nonzero Feshbach correction therefore requires a projector selected from
a reference operator, symmetry, Galerkin space, or approximate coherent
criterion.

For such a reference-selected projector, write
\[
 T=
 \begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ C&D\end{pmatrix}.
\]
At every declared \(z\in\rho(D)\), the exact reduced operator is the
Feshbach--Schur map
\[
 F_T(z)=A-z-B(D-z)^{-1}C.
\]
The omitted-sector self-energy obeys the actual product estimate
\[
 \|B(D-z)^{-1}C\|
 \leq
 \|B\|\,\|(D-z)^{-1}\|\,\|C\|,
\]
and, for self-adjoint \(D\), the middle factor is
\(\dist(z,\spec D)^{-1}\).  We prove the corresponding inverse formula,
give a two-mode example, derive Riesz-projector stability under bounded
perturbations, and establish a local operator-norm robustness bound with
all block variations shown explicitly.

These results define a controlled local class, not a global universality
theorem.  Resolvent closeness does not imply uniform-in-time dynamical
closeness, graph-norm or form robustness requires separate hypotheses, and
an internal one-parameter unitary is not physical time without a
model-specific clock and intertwining theorem.  The result is a rigorous
methods paper and an executable certificate format for future
\MTT{} reductions.
\end{abstract}

\section{The corrected question}
\label{sec:intro}

The original question was:
\begin{quote}
When may the full internal description be replaced by a smaller coherent
sector without losing the behavior relevant to a declared calculation?
\end{quote}
The answer depends on what ``behavior'' means.  A resolvent, a spectral
cluster, a finite-time unitary, a semigroup, a quadratic form, and a list
of observables require different estimates.  No single gap statement
controls all of them automatically.

This paper treats the resolvent and spectral-projector problem first.  It
uses standard Feshbach--Schur and perturbation theory
\cite{Feshbach1962,Kato1995,GriesemerHasler2008}.  The \MTT{} contribution
is a typed certificate that records exactly which version of this standard
mathematics is being used and prevents a local reduction from being
promoted to a universal physical claim.

\subsection{The six objects that must be distinguished}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item A \emph{reference operator} \(G\) may be used to identify a candidate
      coherent subspace.
\item An orthogonal projector \(P\) selects that subspace; \(Q=\1-P\).
\item The \emph{full operator} \(T\) is the object actually being reduced.
\item A spectral parameter \(z\) specifies the resolvent problem.
\item A comparison norm states the sense in which the reduction is
      controlled.
\item A physical interpretation map states whether the reduced parameter
      describes energy, modular flow, Euclidean evolution, or physical
      time.
\end{enumerate}

If \(G=T\) and \(P\) is an exact spectral projector of \(T\), the reduction
has no off-diagonal correction.  If \(P\) is selected from \(G\neq T\), a
finite basis, or another coherent criterion, the coupling to \(Q\cH\)
must be measured.

\subsection{What is not claimed}

The paper does not prove that:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item every \MTT{} encoding has a common coherent projector;
\item a finite projected operator converges to a selected continuum
      operator;
\item operator-norm robustness implies graph-norm, form, or semigroup
      robustness;
\item a resolvent approximation is uniformly accurate for arbitrary
      evolution times;
\item the internal generator is a physical Hamiltonian; or
\item distinct compactifications or microscopic theories are physically
      equivalent merely because one reduced block is close.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why an exact spectral projector gives no correction}
\label{sec:exactP}

Let \(T\) be self-adjoint on a dense domain \(\cD(T)\subset\cH\).  Its
spectral measure is denoted by \(E_T(\cdot)\).

\begin{proposition}[Exact spectral projectors reduce their operator]
\label{prop:reducing}
For every Borel set \(I\subset\RR\), the projector \(P=E_T(I)\) preserves
\(\cD(T)\), commutes with \(T\) on \(\cD(T)\), and satisfies
\[
  PTQ=0,
  \qquad
  QTP=0,
  \qquad Q=\1-P.
\]
Consequently
\[
  T=T_P\oplus T_Q
\]
on \(P\cH\oplus Q\cH\), and truncation to the exact spectral subspace has
zero Feshbach self-energy.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The spectral functional calculus gives
\(E_T(I)E_T(J)=E_T(I\cap J)\) and
\[
  T=\int_{\RR}\lambda\,\dd E_T(\lambda).
\]
Hence \(P\) commutes with every bounded spectral function and preserves
the domain defined by
\(\int\lambda^2\,\dd\langle E_T(\lambda)x,x\rangle<\infty\).
Thus \(PTx=TPx\) for \(x\in\cD(T)\).  Since \(PQ=0\),
\(PTQ=TPQ=0\), and similarly \(QTP=0\).  The direct-sum statement
follows.
\end{proof}

This proposition exposes a hidden circularity in many informal reduction
arguments.  One cannot simultaneously define \(P\) as the exact spectral
projector of \(T\) and then estimate a nonzero coupling \(PTQ\).  To obtain
a nontrivial reduction, at least one of the following must be true:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \(P\) is spectral for a reference operator \(G\), while
      \(T=G+V\);
\item \(P\) is a Galerkin or finite-element projector;
\item \(P\) is selected by symmetry, topology, localization, or another
      criterion not reducing \(T\); or
\item \(P\) approximates, rather than equals, the target spectral
      projector.
\end{enumerate}
The source and status of \(P\) are therefore part of every truncation
certificate.

\section{The closed block-operator problem}
\label{sec:blocks}

Fix an orthogonal decomposition
\[
  \cH=\cH_P\oplus\cH_Q,
  \qquad
  \cH_P=P\cH,\quad \cH_Q=Q\cH.
\]
The following assumptions give a clean theorem while still allowing
unbounded diagonal blocks.

\begin{assumption}[Block domain and bounded coupling]
\label{ass:block}
The operators
\[
 A:\cD(A)\subset\cH_P\to\cH_P,
 \qquad
 D:\cD(D)\subset\cH_Q\to\cH_Q
\]
are self-adjoint.  The off-diagonal maps
\[
 B:\cH_Q\to\cH_P,
 \qquad
 C:\cH_P\to\cH_Q
\]
are bounded and satisfy \(C=B^*\).  The full operator is
\[
 T=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 A&B\\ C&D
 \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \cD(T)=\cD(A)\oplus\cD(D).
\]
\end{assumption}

By the bounded-perturbation theorem, \(T\) is self-adjoint.  This setup is
not the most general Feshbach framework, but its domains and norms are
unambiguous.  Unbounded off-diagonal couplings require relative-bound,
quadratic-form, or graph-norm hypotheses and are outside the theorem below.

\begin{definition}[Declared resolvent region]
A set \(\Omega\subset\CC\) is an eliminated-sector resolvent region if
\[
  \Omega\subset\rho(D)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  r_\Omega:=
  \sup_{z\in\Omega}\|(D-z)^{-1}\|<\infty.
\]
For self-adjoint \(D\),
\[
  \|(D-z)^{-1}\|
  =
  \frac{1}{\dist(z,\spec D)}
\]
for \(z\notin\spec D\).
\end{definition}

A real gap \(\Delta\) is useful only after the location of \(z\) is
declared.  For example, if
\(\dist(\Omega,\spec D)\geq g>0\), then
\(r_\Omega\leq g^{-1}\).  Writing only \(1/\Delta\) hides this dependence.

\section{Exact Feshbach reduction}
\label{sec:feshbach}

\begin{definition}[Feshbach--Schur map]
For \(z\in\rho(D)\), define
\[
  R_D(z)=(D-z)^{-1},
  \qquad
  \Sigma_T(z)=BR_D(z)C,
\]
and
\[
  F_T(z)=A-z-\Sigma_T(z)
\]
on \(\cD(A)\subset\cH_P\).
\end{definition}
The operator \(\Sigma_T(z)\) is the eliminated-sector self-energy.  It is
bounded under \cref{ass:block}; the reduced operator \(F_T(z)\) remains
closed on \(\cD(A)\).

\begin{theorem}[Feshbach inverse formula]
\label{thm:feshbach}
Assume \cref{ass:block} and let \(z\in\rho(D)\).  Then
\[
 z\in\rho(T)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 0\in\rho(F_T(z)).
\]
When these equivalent conditions hold,
\[
(T-z)^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
F_T(z)^{-1}
&
-F_T(z)^{-1}BR_D(z)
\\[0.3em]
-R_D(z)CF_T(z)^{-1}
&
R_D(z)+R_D(z)CF_T(z)^{-1}BR_D(z)
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
In particular,
\[
  P(T-z)^{-1}P=F_T(z)^{-1}
\]
on \(\cH_P\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On \(\cD(A)\oplus\cD(D)\), factor
\[
T-z
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\1&BR_D(z)\\ 0&\1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
F_T(z)&0\\ C&D-z
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The first factor is boundedly invertible.  The second is invertible exactly
when \(F_T(z)\) is invertible, because \(D-z\) is already invertible.  The
inverse of the two triangular factors gives the displayed block formula.
Domain compatibility follows from
\(\operatorname{ran}R_D(z)\subset\cD(D)\).
\end{proof}

The theorem is exact.  It does not say that \(A\) alone is an accurate
replacement.  That approximation is controlled by \(\Sigma_T(z)\).

\begin{corollary}[Actual truncation-error product]
\label{cor:product}
For every \(z\in\rho(D)\),
\[
  \|\Sigma_T(z)\|
  \leq
  \|B\|\,\|R_D(z)\|\,\|C\|.
\]
If \(D\) is self-adjoint, then
\[
  \|\Sigma_T(z)\|
  \leq
  \frac{\|B\|\,\|C\|}
       {\dist(z,\spec D)}.
\]
Under \(C=B^*\), this becomes
\[
  \|\Sigma_T(z)\|
  \leq
  \frac{\|B\|^2}
       {\dist(z,\spec D)}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first inequality is submultiplicativity of the operator norm.  The
second uses the self-adjoint resolvent identity
\(\|R_D(z)\|=\dist(z,\spec D)^{-1}\); the last uses
\(\|B^*\|=\|B\|\).
\end{proof}

The schematic expression \(\|\delta T\|^2/\Delta\) is therefore valid only
after proving that the same bounded perturbation supplies both
off-diagonal blocks and that the declared spectral region remains at
distance at least \(\Delta\) from \(\spec D\).  The product form is the
general statement.

\subsection{A two-mode example}

\begin{example}[One retained and one eliminated mode]
Let
\[
  T=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    a&b\\ \overline b&d
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad a,d\in\RR,
\]
and let \(P\) retain the first coordinate.  For \(z\neq d\),
\[
  F_T(z)
  =
  a-z-\frac{|b|^2}{d-z}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
  \det(T-z)=(d-z)F_T(z).
\]
Thus the self-energy has exact magnitude
\[
  |\Sigma_T(z)|=\frac{|b|^2}{|d-z|}.
\]
The numerator measures coupling to the omitted mode; the denominator
measures spectral distance from it.  If \(P\) were instead the exact
spectral projector of \(T\), the matrix in the corresponding eigenbasis
would be diagonal and \(b=0\).
\end{example}

This example contains the whole mechanism.  A large gap cannot compensate
for an unspecified or unbounded coupling, and a small coupling does not
help at a pole of the eliminated resolvent.

\section{Projector selection and stability}
\label{sec:projector}

Let \(G\) be a self-adjoint reference operator and let a positively
oriented closed contour \(\Gamma\subset\rho(G)\) surround an isolated
spectral cluster.  With the resolvent convention used in this paper, the
Riesz projector is
\[
  P
  =
  -\frac{1}{2\pi i}
  \oint_\Gamma (G-z)^{-1}\,\dd z.
\]
For self-adjoint \(G\), this agrees with the corresponding spectral
projector.

\begin{theorem}[Riesz-projector stability]
\label{thm:riesz}
Let \(V=V^*\) be bounded and set \(G_V=G+V\).  Define
\[
  M_0=\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\|(G-z)^{-1}\|.
\]
If \(M_0\|V\|<1\), then
\(\Gamma\subset\rho(G_V)\), the perturbed projector
\[
  P_V
  =
  -\frac{1}{2\pi i}
  \oint_\Gamma(G_V-z)^{-1}\,\dd z
\]
is well defined, and
\[
\|P_V-P\|
\leq
\frac{\operatorname{length}(\Gamma)}{2\pi}
\frac{M_0^2\|V\|}{1-M_0\|V\|}.
\]
If the right-hand side is less than one and \(P\) has finite rank, then
\(\operatorname{rank}P_V=\operatorname{rank}P\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(z\in\Gamma\),
\[
G_V-z
=
\bigl[\1+V(G-z)^{-1}\bigr](G-z).
\]
The Neumann series gives
\[
\|(G_V-z)^{-1}\|
\leq
\frac{M_0}{1-M_0\|V\|}.
\]
The second resolvent identity gives
\[
(G_V-z)^{-1}-(G-z)^{-1}
=
-(G_V-z)^{-1}V(G-z)^{-1}.
\]
Integrating its norm around \(\Gamma\) yields the displayed bound.  Two
orthogonal projections at distance less than one have isomorphic ranges;
for finite rank their ranks agree.
\end{proof}

This theorem makes projector regularity a conclusion under explicit
conditions, not an independent slogan.  Davis--Kahan subspace-angle
estimates provide related sharp bounds for self-adjoint spectral
subspaces \cite{DavisKahan1970}.

\subsection{What ``bounded geometry'' does not mean here}

In differential geometry, bounded geometry normally includes precise
metric conditions such as a positive injectivity-radius bound and
curvature-derivative bounds.  Bounded commutators in an operator
representation are not, by themselves, equivalent to that notion.

This paper therefore avoids using ``bounded geometry'' as a catch-all
hypothesis.  Its analytic inputs are instead listed directly:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item self-adjointness and domains of \(A\) and \(D\);
\item boundedness of \(B\) and \(C\);
\item a declared common resolvent region;
\item operator-norm perturbations where operator-norm conclusions are
      claimed; and
\item a contour and Neumann margin where projector stability is claimed.
\end{itemize}
If an application uses geometric boundedness to prove these rows, that
geometric theorem belongs in the application.

\section{Local robustness with all blocks visible}
\label{sec:robust}

Consider two block operators \(T_0\) and \(T_1\) on the same decomposition
and domains:
\[
T_j=
\begin{pmatrix}
A_j&B_j\\ C_j&D_j
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad j=0,1.
\]
Let
\[
 R_j(z)=(D_j-z)^{-1},
 \qquad
 \Sigma_j(z)=B_jR_j(z)C_j.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Operator-norm block robustness]
\label{thm:robust}
Suppose \(z\in\rho(D_0)\cap\rho(D_1)\) and
\[
\|R_j(z)\|\leq r,\qquad
\|B_j\|\leq b,\qquad
\|C_j\|\leq c.
\]
Set
\[
\alpha=\|A_1-A_0\|,\quad
\beta=\|B_1-B_0\|,\quad
\gamma=\|C_1-C_0\|,\quad
\delta=\|D_1-D_0\|.
\]
Here the differences are bounded operators on the indicated fixed
spaces.  Then
\[
\|\Sigma_1(z)-\Sigma_0(z)\|
\leq
\beta r c
+b r^2\delta c
+b r\gamma,
\]
and
\[
\|F_{T_1}(z)-F_{T_0}(z)\|
\leq
\alpha+\beta r c+b r^2\delta c+b r\gamma.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Insert and subtract intermediate products:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_1-\Sigma_0
={}&(B_1-B_0)R_1C_1\\
&+B_0(R_1-R_0)C_1\\
&+B_0R_0(C_1-C_0).
\end{aligned}
\]
The resolvent identity gives
\[
  R_1-R_0=-R_1(D_1-D_0)R_0,
\]
so \(\|R_1-R_0\|\leq r^2\delta\).  Submultiplicativity gives the
self-energy bound.  Since
\[
F_{T_1}(z)-F_{T_0}(z)
=(A_1-A_0)-(\Sigma_1-\Sigma_0),
\]
the final estimate follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Common resolvent region from one reference]
\label{cor:neumann}
If \(z\in\rho(D_0)\) and
\[
 \|(D_0-z)^{-1}\|\,\|D_1-D_0\|<1,
\]
then \(z\in\rho(D_1)\) and
\[
\|(D_1-z)^{-1}\|
\leq
\frac{\|(D_0-z)^{-1}\|}
 {1-\|(D_0-z)^{-1}\|\,\|D_1-D_0\|}.
\]
\end{corollary}

This is the precise local content of robustness in the present paper.  It
does not cover changing domains, relatively bounded unbounded
perturbations, form convergence, strong-resolvent convergence, or
semigroup convergence.  Those are different topologies with different
theorems \cite{Kato1995,ReedSimonI1980}.

\section{What ``universality'' can mean}
\label{sec:universality}

Version 1 called two microscopic geometries universal when their effective
generators agreed up to controlled error.  Approximate agreement at one
tolerance is not automatically an equivalence relation: errors accumulate
under composition.

\begin{definition}[Reduced-resolvent comparison functional]
Let two controlled records have retained spaces
\(\cH_{P,0}\) and \(\cH_{P,1}\) of equal Hilbert dimension and a common
region
\[
  \Omega\subset
  \rho(T_0)\cap\rho(D_0)\cap\rho(T_1)\cap\rho(D_1).
\]
Define
\[
d_\Omega(T_0,T_1)
=
\inf_U\;
\sup_{z\in\Omega}
\left\|
 U F_{T_0}(z)^{-1}U^*-F_{T_1}(z)^{-1}
\right\|,
\]
where the infimum is over declared unitaries
\(U:\cH_{P,0}\to\cH_{P,1}\).  If no such identification is supplied, set
\(d_\Omega=\infty\).
\end{definition}

The inverse formula identifies each bounded term in this definition with
the compressed resolvent \(P_j(T_j-z)^{-1}P_j\).  We call \(d_\Omega\) a
comparison functional rather than a metric: an application may restrict
the allowed unitaries, and metric axioms then require separate closure
conditions on that class.
An \(\varepsilon\)-neighborhood under \(d_\Omega\) is a useful local
comparison class.  Exact zero comparison can define a reduced
equivalence after quotienting declared presentation redundancies.  Finite
\(\varepsilon\)-closeness is tolerance dependent and should be reported as
such.

The paper therefore uses \emph{local universality} only for a declared
neighborhood in a declared reduced metric.  It makes no claim that all
viable quantum, gravitational, or compactification models occupy one
class.

\section{Resolvent control is not uniform dynamical control}
\label{sec:dynamics}

The Feshbach map is naturally an energy- or spectral-parameter-dependent
object.  Additional work is required to turn it into a statement about
\(e^{-itT}\).

\begin{proposition}[A finite-time Duhamel bound]
\label{prop:duhamel}
Let \(T=T_{\rm diag}+W\) be bounded and self-adjoint, where
\[
T_{\rm diag}=A\oplus D
\]
and \(W\) contains the off-diagonal blocks.  Then
\[
\|e^{-itT}-e^{-itT_{\rm diag}}\|
\leq |t|\,\|W\|.
\]
Consequently
\[
\|Pe^{-itT}P-e^{-itA}P\|
\leq |t|\,\|W\|.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Duhamel's formula gives
\[
e^{-itT}-e^{-itT_{\rm diag}}
=
-i\int_0^t
e^{-i(t-s)T}W e^{-isT_{\rm diag}}\,\dd s.
\]
Both unitary factors have norm one, so integration gives the bound.
Compression by \(P\) cannot increase the norm.
\end{proof}

This estimate is first order in the coupling and grows with the time
horizon.  Gap-improved adiabatic, Schrieffer--Wolff, or superadiabatic
estimates are possible under additional hypotheses
\cite{BravyiDiVincenzoLoss2011}, but they are not consequences of
\cref{cor:product} alone.

\begin{example}[No uniform-in-time conclusion from a small generator error]
On the one-dimensional Hilbert space let \(H_0=0\) and
\(H_\varepsilon=\varepsilon\).  Their generators differ by
\(\varepsilon\), and their resolvents are close on compact sets away from
zero.  Yet at \(t=\pi/\varepsilon\),
\[
\|e^{-itH_\varepsilon}-e^{-itH_0}\|=2.
\]
Thus small operator or resolvent error does not imply uniform closeness for
arbitrarily long times.
\end{example}

\section{An internal generator is not automatically physical time}
\label{sec:time}

Every self-adjoint \(T\) generates a strongly continuous unitary group
\[
  U_T(s)=e^{-isT}
\]
by Stone's theorem \cite{ReedSimonI1980}.  This mathematical parameter
\(s\) need not be the time read by a physical clock.  It might label a
modular flow, an internal phase, a Euclidean interpolation, or an auxiliary
spectral transformation.

A physical-time claim requires additional data such as:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item a physical state and observable algebra;
\item a clock or operational time protocol;
\item a physical Hamiltonian \(H_{\rm phys}\);
\item an embedding or decoding map
      \(J:\cH_{\rm phys}\to\cH_P\); and
\item an exact or controlled intertwining relation, for example
\[
  J e^{-itH_{\rm phys}}
  =
  e^{-i\theta(t)A_{\rm eff}}J
  +E(t),
  \qquad
  \|E(t)\|\leq\varepsilon(t),
\]
on a declared interval and domain.
\end{enumerate}
Without these rows, this paper establishes spectral reduction, not the
emergence of observed time.

\section{A complete controlled-reduction certificate}
\label{sec:certificate}

\begin{definition}[Controlled coherent-sector certificate]
A controlled coherent-sector certificate on \(\Omega\) contains:
\[
\mathfrak C_{\rm red}
=
\bigl(
\cH,G,T,P,Q,
\cD(A),\cD(D),
A,B,C,D,
\Omega,r_\Omega,
\mathfrak n,\varepsilon,
\pi
\bigr),
\]
where \(\mathfrak n\) names the comparison norm,
\(\varepsilon\) is the proved error budget, and \(\pi\) records the
provenance and status of every input.
\end{definition}

At minimum, the certificate should answer:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item What selected \(P\), and is it exact, approximate, or finite?
\item Which operator is being reduced?
\item Are the block domains fixed and dense?
\item Is each off-diagonal block bounded, relatively bounded, or only
      form bounded?
\item What is the spectral region \(\Omega\)?
\item How is \(\Omega\subset\rho(D)\) certified?
\item Which norm or topology is controlled?
\item Is the approximation \(A\), \(F_T(z)\), or a further
      energy-independent operator?
\item What parameter range preserves the projector rank and resolvent
      margin?
\item What observable or physical interpretation is attached afterward?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Finite and numerical execution}

For a finite matrix or Galerkin truncation, an executable certificate can
report:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item exact dimensions and basis hashes;
\item Hermiticity residuals and interval enclosures;
\item the projector source and
      \(\|(I-P)GP\|\);
\item interval bounds for \(\spec D\) or singular values of \(D-z\);
\item certified norms of \(B\), \(C\), and \(R_D(z)\);
\item the self-energy product and the resulting reduced resolvent error;
\item projector-angle or contour bounds under perturbation; and
\item roundoff, discretization, and continuum-transfer errors as separate
      rows.
\end{itemize}
The finite calculation is exact for the finite object if performed with
exact or interval-certified arithmetic.  It becomes a continuum theorem
only after a convergence and domain-transfer result is supplied.

\section{Relation to MTT and established mathematics}
\label{sec:relation}

\subsection{Established operator theory}

The Schur complement and Feshbach map are standard tools.  Their
isospectral and inverse properties are established in operator-theoretic
forms, including smooth variants
\cite{Feshbach1962,GriesemerHasler2008}.  Spectral projections, closed
operators, bounded perturbations, and resolvent convergence belong to
classical perturbation theory \cite{Kato1995,ReedSimonI1980}.
Davis--Kahan theory controls rotations of self-adjoint spectral subspaces
\cite{DavisKahan1970}.

Accordingly, this paper does not claim the Feshbach formula or Riesz
projection as new \MTT{} mathematics.  Its contribution is the correction
and integration:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item exact spectral projectors are separated from reference-selected
      projectors;
\item the actual product bound replaces a proxy slogan;
\item every theorem states its domain, parameter, norm, and perturbation
      class;
\item local reduction is not relabeled as global universality; and
\item spectral flow is not relabeled as physical time.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{MTT ownership and use}

The \MTT{} Foundation owns the general admissibility ledger and status
semantics \cite{MTTFoundation2026}.  The normalized-capacity paper owns
the rowwise slack, scale, provenance, and bottleneck conventions
\cite{CapacityMargin2026}.  This paper owns only the coherent-sector
operator certificate, the exact-projector correction, the domain-explicit
Feshbach specialization, the local block-robustness estimate, and the
resolvent-versus-time boundary.

Later \MTT{} papers may cite this certificate, but they must still construct
their own \(G,T,P\), prove the relevant domain and resolvent rows, and
attach a physical interpretation map.  Citation does not fill those
objects.

\section{Completion and falsifiability contract}
\label{sec:completion}

A model-specific \MTT{} application is complete at the controlled
resolvent-reduction tier only if it supplies:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item a selected, reproducible reference operator \(G\);
\item a projector \(P\) with rank and stability certificate;
\item the full operator \(T\) and all block domains;
\item verified off-diagonal maps \(B\) and \(C\);
\item a nonempty spectral region \(\Omega\subset\rho(D)\);
\item a certified resolvent margin and self-energy product;
\item a reduced operator and declared comparison norm;
\item a numerical or analytic error budget;
\item a continuum-transfer theorem if the source is finite; and
\item a physical observation or time map if physical dynamics is claimed.
\end{enumerate}

The reduction fails in its declared regime if the selected rank changes,
the contour meets the spectrum, the eliminated resolvent loses its bound,
the off-diagonal product exceeds the tolerance, the domain assumptions
fail, or a held-out observable lies outside the certified error budget.
Those failure modes make the framework testable rather than merely
descriptive.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Coherent-sector reduction is a precise operator problem, not a consequence
of the word ``coherence.''  If the retained projector is exactly spectral
for the full operator, the sector already reduces the operator and there
is no self-energy.  If the projector comes from a reference or
approximation, the omitted sector contributes the exact
Feshbach--Schur term \(B(D-z)^{-1}C\).

The resulting control has three visible ingredients: coupling into the
omitted sector, resolvent distance within that sector, and coupling back.
Projector stability follows from a separate contour and perturbation
margin.  Robustness is local to the norm, domains, region, and block
variations actually bounded.

This corrected formulation gives \MTT{} a reusable and rigorous reduction
certificate.  It does not yet identify a universal coherent projector,
derive physical time, or prove equivalence of different microscopic
theories.  Those stronger statements require model-specific source,
continuum, dynamics, and observation maps.
