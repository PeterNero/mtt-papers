\begin{abstract}
We determine exactly what a nil-type termination of an admissible description
does and does not imply.  Failure of a declared chart or continuation map does
not by itself collapse nearby admissible states to a discrete set.  A finite
survivor set follows, for example, when a smooth constraint map
$C:M^n\to\mathbb R^n$ is transverse to zero and its zero set is compact.
Discrete spectral values follow under a different hypothesis: a self-adjoint
operator on a Hilbert space has discrete finite-multiplicity spectrum when its
resolvent is compact.  Neither conclusion follows from the nil label alone.

These results define a useful MTT \emph{discrete survivor filter}, but they do
not uniquely characterize quantum mechanics.  Finite classical state
machines, Morse critical sets, symbolic labels, and topological winding
sectors also give discrete survivors.  Moreover, discreteness does not supply
a noncommutative observable algebra, a complex Hilbert space, canonical
commutation or anticommutation relations, dynamics, measurement instruments,
or the Born rule.  Indeed, exact finite-dimensional canonical commutation
relations are impossible by the trace of a commutator.

We therefore formulate quantum mechanics as a conditional reconstruction.
Given a complex unital C*-algebra, a positive normalized state, a
representation or the associated GNS construction, a dynamical law, and
effects or instruments, the standard operator and probability structures are
available.  Program B3 establishes the discrete-filter and compact-resolvent
bridges and records the remaining same-source obligations; it does not derive
complex quantum mechanics from nil termination.
\end{abstract}

\section{How to Read Program B3}

The word ``quantization'' is used for several mathematically different
operations.  A continuous model may acquire discrete labels because a
constraint has isolated solutions, because a quotient has collapsed an orbit,
because an operator has discrete spectrum, or because a quantum observable is
represented on a Hilbert space.  These mechanisms can coexist, but none of
them is interchangeable with the others.  The purpose of this paper is to
separate them before asking whether MTT supplies a quantum theory.

\subsection{The four layers of the argument}

The discussion is organized as a sequence of four gates.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Termination.}  A declared chart or decoder ceases to extend.
This is the nil-type statement.  It concerns the description being used, not
the cardinality of the underlying state space.
\item \textbf{Survival.}  A constraint, controlled domain, and equivalence
relation select the states that remain admissible.  Transversality and
compactness can then make the reduced survivor set finite.
\item \textbf{Spectrum.}  A separately supplied operator can have discrete
spectrum.  Compact resolvent is one standard sufficient condition, but it
already presupposes a Hilbert carrier and operator-domain data.
\item \textbf{Quantum reconstruction.}  A complex observable algebra, a
state, a representation, dynamics, and measurement effects are supplied from
one source.  Only at this layer do Hilbert-space amplitudes and Born
probabilities become available.
\end{enumerate}
The main logical lesson is therefore
\[
 \text{nil termination}
 \;\not\Rightarrow\;
 \text{finite survivors}
 \;\not\Rightarrow\;
 \text{discrete spectrum}
 \;\not\Rightarrow\;
 \text{quantum mechanics}.
\]
Each arrow can become valid only after the missing hypotheses displayed later
in the paper are added.

\subsection{Object picture: a chart can end while states continue}

Imagine describing a smooth road with a coordinate that becomes infinite at a
particular marker.  The coordinate description has failed there, but the road
has not become a collection of isolated points.  Definition~\ref{def:nil}
formalizes exactly this relative kind of failure.  To obtain isolated
survivors one must add an actual selection rule, such as simultaneous
constraints whose Jacobian has full rank.

This distinction is especially important in MTT.  The circle, lens, and nil
profiles describe how a chosen representation behaves.  A nil profile may
tell us where a continuation is unavailable, while a lens profile may identify
many representatives of one reduced state.  Neither profile, without a
declared source map, chooses an observable algebra or a probability measure.

\subsection{What the reader should take from each theorem}

The regular-value results explain when geometric constraints isolate
survivors.  The classical countermodels show that isolation is not uniquely
quantum.  The compact-resolvent theorem explains a different route to
discreteness through operator theory.  The commutator no-go prevents a finite
matrix model from being mistaken for an exact bosonic canonical system.
Finally, the GNS, Born-evaluation, and Stone results show precisely what
standard quantum structure follows once the corresponding algebraic data have
been supplied.  They are reconstruction results, not claims that nil
termination generated those data.

\section{Scope and Imported Data}

Program A0 supplies typed reductions, admissible domains, exact factorization
criteria, and controlled approximate descent~\cite{A0}.  Program B0 treats
circle, lens, and nil as coarse profiles rather than an exhaustive
classification or a list of literal topological factors~\cite{B0}.  In that
usage, a nil profile records failure of a declared chart, decoder, transition,
or continuation to extend.  Programs B1 and B2 show how physical gravity and
gauge theory arise only after their own geometric and dynamical data are
supplied~\cite{B1,B2}.

The present paper asks:
\begin{quote}
Under which additional hypotheses does a nil-adjacent admissibility problem
have discrete survivors, and what further structures are needed before those
survivors constitute quantum mechanics?
\end{quote}

The distinction is essential.  The empty fiber of one reduction can coexist
with a perfectly continuous upper evolution or with a different admissible
chart.  Even when the selected survivor set is finite, a finite set is not a
Hilbert space, an observable algebra, a probability law, or a quantum
measurement theory.

\section{Nil Profiles and Survivor Data}

\subsection{Typed termination}

\begin{definition}[Nil-type profile]\label{def:nil}
Let $X$ be an upper state space, let $D\subseteq X$ be the domain of a declared
reduction or chart
\[
 \pi:D\longrightarrow Y,
\]
and let $\mathcal C$ be a declared class of admissible extensions.  A point
$x\in\overline D$ has a \emph{nil-type profile relative to
$(\pi,D,\mathcal C)$} when $\pi$ has no extension in $\mathcal C$ to any
allowed neighborhood of $x$.
\end{definition}

This is a relative predicate.  It depends on the map, its domain, the allowed
category, and the extension class.  It does not say that $x$ is absent from
$X$, that upper dynamics terminates, or that every other chart fails.

\begin{proposition}[Termination does not imply discreteness]
\label{prop:nil-not-discrete}
A nil-type profile in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:nil} does not imply
that the admissible states in $D$, in $\overline D$, or in a neighboring chart
form a discrete set.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $X=\mathbb R$, $D=(-\infty,0)$, $Y=\mathbb R$, and
\[
 \pi(x)=\frac{1}{x}.
\]
In the category of finite continuous real-valued maps, $\pi$ has no extension
to a neighborhood of $0$, so $0$ has a nil-type profile.  Nevertheless $D$ is
a continuum.  Failure of the selected decoder therefore does not collapse
the upper or admissible domain to isolated points.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The example is deliberately elementary.  The same logical separation applies
to singular coordinate systems, blow-up charts, finite-band decoders, and
effective descriptions whose conditioning fails at a boundary.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A separate survivor predicate}

\begin{definition}[Survivor datum]\label{def:survivor}
A \emph{survivor datum} is a tuple
\[
 (M,C,K,\sim),
\]
where $M$ is a declared state or parameter space, $C:M\to V$ is a constraint
map into a declared target, $K\subseteq M$ is the controlled domain, and
$\sim$ is the declared equivalence relation.  Its raw and reduced survivor
sets are
\[
 \Surv_{\rm raw}=K\cap C^{-1}(0),
 \qquad
 \Surv=\Surv_{\rm raw}/\sim.
\]
\end{definition}

The nil profile can motivate the choice of $C$ or $K$, but it does not
determine them.  Discreteness is a theorem about this survivor datum.

\begin{definition}[Discrete survivor filter]
A survivor datum is a \emph{discrete survivor filter} when its reduced
survivor set $\Surv$ is discrete in the declared quotient topology.  It is a
\emph{finite survivor filter} when $\Surv$ is finite.
\end{definition}

The quotient must be stated.  A continuous gauge orbit can represent one
reduced survivor, while an unquotiented presentation remains continuous.

\section{When Constraints Actually Give Discrete Survivors}

\subsection{The transverse compact theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Regular survivor dimension]\label{thm:regular-value}
Let $M$ be a smooth $n$-manifold, let
$C:M\to\mathbb R^r$ be smooth, and suppose that $0$ is a regular value of
$C$.  Then
\[
 C^{-1}(0)
\]
is either empty or a smooth submanifold of dimension $n-r$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
This is the regular-value theorem: surjectivity of
$dC_x:T_xM\to\mathbb R^r$ for every $x\in C^{-1}(0)$ supplies local
coordinates in which $C^{-1}(0)$ is cut out by $r$ coordinate equations
~\cite{Lee}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Compact transverse finite-survivor theorem]
\label{cor:finite}
Under the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:regular-value}, if $r=n$ and
$C^{-1}(0)$ is compact, then $C^{-1}(0)$ is finite.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The regular-value theorem makes $C^{-1}(0)$ a zero-dimensional manifold, hence
a discrete topological space.  A compact discrete space is finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What each hypothesis does]
Transversality gives local isolation.  Equal source and constraint dimensions
give dimension zero.  Compactness rules out infinitely many isolated solutions
escaping to infinity.  None of these properties is contained in the word
``nil.''
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why dimension counting alone is insufficient}

If $r<n$, a regular survivor set has positive dimension $n-r$ and therefore
retains continuous families.  If $r>n$, transversality to zero forces an empty
preimage.  If zero is singular, the preimage can be discrete, continuous,
stratified, or nonreduced.  For example,
\[
 C(x,y)=x^2
\]
has the continuous zero set $\{0\}\times\mathbb R$ even though one equation
vanishes to higher order.  Thus a count of equations is only informative when
rank and domain hypotheses are certified.

\subsection{Quotient survivors}

\begin{proposition}[Finite raw set implies finite reduced set]
\label{prop:quotient-finite}
If $\Surv_{\rm raw}$ is finite, then
$\Surv_{\rm raw}/\sim$ is finite for every equivalence relation $\sim$.
The converse need not hold.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A quotient map cannot create more equivalence classes than there are elements.
For the converse, a nontrivial group can act transitively on an infinite or
continuous raw set, producing a one-point quotient.
\end{proof}

This proposition prevents a common conflation: reduced discreteness can result
from a quotient rather than from isolated upper states.

\subsection{Robustness under perturbation}

\begin{proposition}[Local persistence of a transverse survivor]
\label{prop:persistence}
Let $C_\lambda:M^n\to\mathbb R^n$ depend smoothly on a finite-dimensional
parameter $\lambda$.  If $C_0(x_0)=0$ and $dC_0|_{x_0}$ is invertible, then
for all sufficiently small $\lambda$ there is a unique nearby smooth branch
$x(\lambda)$ with $C_\lambda(x(\lambda))=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the implicit-function theorem to $(x,\lambda)\mapsto C_\lambda(x)$ at
$(x_0,0)$.
\end{proof}

The proposition gives a precise replacement for the former phrase ``stable
under arbitrary refinement.''  Stability must be tied to a topology,
perturbation class, and nonsingularity margin.

\section{Classical Sources of Discrete Survivors}

Discrete labels are not unique to quantum theory.

\subsection{Finite deterministic systems}

Let $F:S\to S$ be a deterministic map on a finite set.  Its states, cycles,
basins, and transition graph are all discrete.  No complex amplitude,
noncommutative observable algebra, or Born rule is present.  This is already a
counterexample to the implication
\[
 \text{discrete survivors}\Longrightarrow\text{quantum mechanics}.
\]

\subsection{Morse critical points}

Let $M$ be compact and let $f:M\to\mathbb R$ be a Morse function.  Its
critical points are isolated and therefore finite.  They are the zeros of
$df$, with nondegenerate Hessian, and are robust in the usual Morse-theoretic
sense.  This is a smooth classical realization of
Corollary~\ref{cor:finite}.

\subsection{Symbolic dynamics}

A finite alphabet supplies discrete symbols and a finite directed graph
supplies discrete admissibility rules.  Bi-infinite symbolic trajectories can
nevertheless form an uncountable compact space.  Thus ``discrete alphabet''
does not even imply a discrete trajectory space.  Symbolic dynamics illustrates
both the usefulness and the limits of discrete encodings~\cite{LindMarcus}.

\subsection{Topological sectors}

Maps $S^1\to S^1$ are classified up to homotopy by an integer degree.  The
sector label is discrete, although each sector contains continuous families of
maps.  Likewise, characteristic classes and winding numbers can label
components without quantizing all local degrees of freedom~\cite{Hatcher}.

\begin{remark}[Conclusion from the countermodels]
A discrete survivor filter is a broad structural response available to
classical, topological, combinatorial, and quantum models.  It is canonical
only at the level of the declared constraint problem, not as a unique
identification with quantum mechanics.
\end{remark}

Taken together, these examples explain why the survivor theorem should be
read as a reusable geometric tool rather than as a definition of the quantum.
The same theorem can organize classical equilibria, topological sectors, or
candidate outcome labels.  What those labels mean physically is decided only
by the additional source and representation data attached to them.

\section{Spectral Discreteness Is a Separate Theorem}

\subsection{Compact resolvent}

\begin{definition}[Compact resolvent]
Let $A$ be a densely defined closed operator on a Hilbert space $\Hilb$ with
nonempty resolvent set.  It has \emph{compact resolvent} if
\[
 (A-zI)^{-1}
\]
is compact for one, and hence every, $z$ in the resolvent set.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Self-adjoint compact-resolvent spectrum]
\label{thm:compact-resolvent}
Let $A$ be self-adjoint on a complex Hilbert space and have compact resolvent.
Then $\spec(A)\subset\mathbb R$ consists only of isolated eigenvalues of finite
multiplicity, with no finite accumulation point.  If $\Hilb$ is
infinite-dimensional, the eigenvalues can accumulate only at infinity
~\cite{ReedSimon,Conway}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $z\notin\mathbb R$, the resolvent
$R_z=(A-zI)^{-1}$ is compact and normal.  The spectral theorem for compact
normal operators gives nonzero eigenvalues of finite multiplicity whose only
possible accumulation point is zero.  The spectral mapping
$\mu=(\lambda-z)^{-1}$ transfers these values to eigenvalues of $A$ and turns
accumulation at zero into escape of $|\lambda|$ to infinity.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What the theorem imports}

Theorem~\ref{thm:compact-resolvent} assumes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a Hilbert space;
\item a densely defined operator and its domain;
\item closedness and self-adjointness;
\item a nonempty resolvent set; and
\item compactness of the resolvent.
\end{enumerate}
A nil-type chart failure supplies none of these.  Conversely, a finite matrix
has a finite spectrum for elementary algebraic reasons, even if it came from a
classical discretization.  A finite Galerkin truncation therefore cannot, by
itself, prove compact resolvent or the spectrum of its continuum parent.

\subsection{Topological and spectral discreteness differ}

A topological sector label classifies connected components or homotopy classes.
A spectral value is an eigenvalue of a declared operator.  Either can be
discrete while the other is absent.  Identifying them requires an explicit map
from the topological or constraint data to the operator and a theorem that
preserves the relevant spectrum.

\section{The Quantum Structures That Discreteness Does Not Supply}

\subsection{Seven logically separate layers}

For this paper, a quantum reconstruction distinguishes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a survivor or outcome label set;
\item a complex linear state carrier;
\item a noncommutative involutive observable algebra;
\item a representation of that algebra;
\item canonical relations, when relevant;
\item dynamics; and
\item states, effects, instruments, and probabilities.
\end{enumerate}

No implication from item~1 to items~2--7 is valid without additional
hypotheses.

\subsection{Commutativity is not decided by a set}

For a finite set $S$, the algebra $C(S)$ of complex functions is commutative.
The matrix algebra $M_N(\mathbb C)$ is noncommutative.  Both may be built over
the same number $N$ of labels.  Therefore cardinality or discreteness cannot
select the observable product.

\subsection{Complex amplitudes are not decided by a circle}

A supplied Hermitian line bundle with $U(1)$ connection can carry phase and
holonomy.  It does not by itself select a complex Hilbert space of physical
states, an inner product, linear superposition, or a representation of
observables.  Real and quaternionic quantum formalisms also show that the
scalar field is an independent structural choice.  A shared MTT circle may
participate in a complex reconstruction only through a proved connection-
preserving source map.

\subsection{Finite-dimensional CCR no-go}

\begin{theorem}[No exact finite-dimensional canonical commutator]
\label{thm:ccr-no-go}
Let $Q,P\in M_N(\mathbb C)$ and let $\hbar\ne0$.  Then
\[
 [Q,P]=i\hbar I_N
\]
is impossible.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The cyclicity of the finite-dimensional trace gives
\[
 \operatorname{tr}[Q,P]
 =\operatorname{tr}(QP)-\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=0.
\]
The proposed right-hand side has trace $i\hbar N\ne0$.
\end{proof}

Exact bosonic CCR representations therefore require an infinite-dimensional
setting and careful unbounded-operator domains, or a Weyl-algebra formulation.
A finite survivor matrix may approximate selected observables, but it is not
an exact CCR representation merely because it has discrete states.

\subsection{CAR is also an input}

For finitely many fermionic modes, the CAR algebra has finite-dimensional
matrix representations.  This possibility does not make CAR follow from
finiteness.  One must still supply generators $a_j,a_j^*$ and prove
\[
 \{a_j,a_k^*\}=\delta_{jk}I,
 \qquad
 \{a_j,a_k\}=0.
\]
The distinction between the CCR no-go and finite-mode CAR representations is
another reason to keep ``discrete'' and ``quantum'' separate.

\section{Conditional C*-Algebraic Quantum Reconstruction}

\subsection{Reconstruction datum}

\begin{assumption}[Quantum reconstruction datum]\label{ass:quantum}
Supply:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a complex unital C*-algebra $\Alg$ of observables;
\item a state $\omega:\Alg\to\mathbb C$, meaning a positive linear functional
with $\omega(I)=1$;
\item a strongly continuous dynamical law, either a one-parameter group
$\alpha_t$ of *-automorphisms or a declared open-system evolution;
\item a class of effects $0\le E\le I$ and, for sequential measurements, a
specified instrument; and
\item a source map relating the selected MTT survivor, bundle, or operator data
to $\Alg$, $\omega$, the dynamics, and the effects.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\subsection{GNS representation}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional Hilbert reconstruction]\label{thm:gns}
Under the first two items of Assumption~\ref{ass:quantum}, there exist a complex
Hilbert space $\Hilb_\omega$, a unital *-representation
\[
 \pi_\omega:\Alg\longrightarrow B(\Hilb_\omega),
\]
and a cyclic unit vector $\Omega_\omega$ such that
\[
 \omega(A)
 =\langle\Omega_\omega,\pi_\omega(A)\Omega_\omega\rangle
 \qquad(A\in\Alg).
\]
The cyclic representation is unique up to unitary equivalence
~\cite{Murphy,BratteliRobinson}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On $\Alg$, define
$\langle A,B\rangle_\omega=\omega(A^*B)$ and quotient by the null left ideal
$N_\omega=\{A:\omega(A^*A)=0\}$.  Complete the quotient to
$\Hilb_\omega$.  Left multiplication defines $\pi_\omega$, and the class of
$I$ defines $\Omega_\omega$.  The standard cyclicity and uniqueness argument
gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The theorem reconstructs a representation from the supplied pair
$(\Alg,\omega)$.  It does not select either member of that pair from a
survivor set.
\end{remark}

In practical terms, GNS is a translator.  It turns algebraic expectation data
into vectors and operators on a Hilbert space.  It does not manufacture the
expectation functional, decide which algebra describes the experiment, or
identify one survivor with one physical outcome.  Those are precisely the
same-source tasks retained in Definition~\ref{def:certificate}.

\subsection{Conditional Born probabilities}

\begin{proposition}[Probabilities after state and effect are supplied]
\label{prop:born}
For every effect $E\in\Alg$,
\[
 p_\omega(E)=\omega(E)
\]
lies in $[0,1]$.  In the GNS representation,
\[
 p_\omega(E)
 =\langle\Omega_\omega,\pi_\omega(E)\Omega_\omega\rangle.
\]
For a vector state and a projection, this is the usual Born expression.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Positivity gives $\omega(E)\ge0$.  Since $I-E\ge0$,
$1-\omega(E)=\omega(I-E)\ge0$.  The GNS identity gives the second formula.
\end{proof}

This is a probability evaluation theorem, not a probability source theorem.
It does not derive $\omega$, the effect $E$, a detector, a capture process, or
one realized outcome.

\subsection{Dynamics and Stone's theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Conditional Hamiltonian generator]\label{thm:stone}
Let $U:\mathbb R\to\mathcal U(\Hilb)$ be a strongly continuous one-parameter
unitary group.  There is a unique self-adjoint operator $H$ such that
\[
 U(t)=e^{-itH}
\]
~\cite{Stone}.
\end{theorem}

A C*-dynamical system $(\Alg,\alpha_t)$ is unitarily implemented in the GNS
representation of an invariant state under the standard construction.  The
existence of dynamics, invariance of the state, and the physical normalization
of $H$ remain additional inputs; a discrete spectrum does not choose them.

\subsection{Measurement and records}

An effect assigns a probability to one event.  A quantum instrument additionally
specifies outcome probabilities and state updates.  Repeatability, decoherence,
records, detector thresholds, and objective history selection are further
dynamical statements.  Nil termination alone proves none of them.  In
particular, ``one survivor remains'' is not a collapse theorem unless the
physical instrument and state-update map have been derived.

\section{The MTT Quantum Source Contract}

\subsection{Same-source requirement}

An MTT realization reaches quantum-mechanical reconstruction only if one
selected upper source emits compatible instances of all the structures in
Assumption~\ref{ass:quantum}.  Combining an independently chosen survivor set,
an unrelated matrix algebra, an observed probability vector, and a fitted
Hamiltonian is a profile replay, not a source theorem.

\begin{definition}[MTT quantum source certificate]\label{def:certificate}
An \emph{MTT quantum source certificate} consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a hash-addressed upper carrier and admissibility domain;
\item a survivor or constraint operator with a proved domain and quotient;
\item a complex C*-algebra and representation emitted from that carrier;
\item selected CCR, CAR, or finite observable relations with exact or
controlled-error certificates;
\item selected dynamics with domain, self-adjointness or complete-positivity
conditions, and normalization;
\item a positive normalized state and selected effects or instruments;
\item a commuting source map connecting the upper data to each lower object;
and
\item an independent verifier that distinguishes exact, controlled,
profile-replay, and open rows.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\subsection{What current finite carriers can establish}

A finite projected algebra can be an exact object when the theory explicitly
selects that finite algebra.  It can establish finite spectral data,
noncommutative matrix products, finite-mode CAR, and exact finite traces.
It cannot establish exact bosonic CCR by
Theorem~\ref{thm:ccr-no-go}.  Nor does an exact finite trace automatically
become the physical quantum state or detector probability.  Those
identifications require the source certificate of
Definition~\ref{def:certificate}.

\subsection{Relation to circle and lens profiles}

The profiles can coexist:
\begin{itemize}
\item circle-type transport can act as phase or connection data;
\item lens-type quotienting can encode redundant representatives; and
\item a nil-adjacent constraint can select a survivor sector.
\end{itemize}
Compatibility of these roles is not automatic commutativity.  Their actions,
domains, quotient order, and connection transport must be supplied and checked.
No exhaustive ``one profile, one physical theory'' triad is asserted.

\section{Scoped B3 Theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Discrete filters and conditional quantum reconstruction]
\label{thm:scoped}
For the typed data and hypotheses of this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
\item failure of a declared chart or decoder to extend does not by itself imply
a discrete state or survivor set;
\item a smooth constraint $C:M^n\to\mathbb R^n$ transverse to zero has isolated
zeros, and a compact zero set is finite;
\item classical finite-state, Morse, symbolic, and topological systems can
produce discrete survivor labels;
\item a self-adjoint operator with compact resolvent has discrete
finite-multiplicity spectrum, but that conclusion imports a Hilbert space,
operator domain, self-adjointness, and compactness;
\item a discrete set selects neither a noncommutative algebra nor complex
amplitudes;
\item exact finite-dimensional bosonic CCR are impossible, while finite-mode
CAR still require an independently supplied algebra;
\item a complex C*-algebra and state admit the GNS representation;
\item Born probabilities follow for supplied effects and a supplied state; and
\item a Hamiltonian generator follows from supplied strongly continuous
unitary dynamics.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently, a discrete survivor filter is a useful nil-type response but is
not a derivation or unique characterization of complex quantum mechanics.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Item~1 is Proposition~\ref{prop:nil-not-discrete}.  Item~2 is
Theorem~\ref{thm:regular-value} and Corollary~\ref{cor:finite}.  Item~3 is
established by the countermodels above.  Item~4 is
Theorem~\ref{thm:compact-resolvent}.  Item~5 follows from the commutative and
matrix-algebra counterexamples and the independent scalar-field choice.
Item~6 is Theorem~\ref{thm:ccr-no-go} and the subsequent CAR discussion.
Items~7--9 are Theorem~\ref{thm:gns},
Proposition~\ref{prop:born}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:stone}.
\end{proof}

\item records classical finite-state, Morse, symbolic, and topological

\item separates topological-sector discreteness from operator-spectrum

\item states the compact-resolvent spectral theorem with all imported
hypotheses;
\item separates outcome labels, complex state carriers, observable algebras,
representations, canonical relations, dynamics, and probability;
\item proves that exact finite-dimensional bosonic CCR are impossible;
\item moves complex Hilbert, C*-algebraic, CCR/CAR, dynamics, measurement, and
Born claims into independent reconstruction theorems; and
\item withdraws the claims that quantization is the unique response to nil or
that the circle--lens--nil profiles exhaust physical gravity, gauge theory, and
quantum mechanics.
\end{itemize}

The next source theorem must provide one selected MTT carrier satisfying
Definition~\ref{def:certificate}.  In particular, it must emit the observable
algebra, state, representation, dynamics, and effects from the same source as
the survivor filter, with exact or controlled transport between them.  A
finite matrix or a discrete label set alone does not close this frontier.

\section{Conclusion}

Nil-type termination remains a useful warning: a declared reduced description
has reached the end of its admissible domain.  It can motivate a search for
robust survivor data.  The revised mathematics shows, however, that the
survivors become discrete only when an actual constraint or spectral theorem
makes them discrete.

That distinction strengthens the MTT program.  The compact transverse theorem
gives an exact finite-survivor route, and the compact-resolvent theorem gives an
exact spectral route.  Their hypotheses can be tested.  Classical
countermodels then show where the quantum claim begins: complex linearity,
noncommutative observables, canonical relations, dynamics, states, and
measurement effects must all be sourced.

Program B3 therefore supplies a rigorous discrete-filter layer and a clear
quantum reconstruction contract.  It does not call every discrete structure
quantum.  A later selected-source theorem can now succeed by filling explicit
mathematical slots rather than by relying on the ambiguity of the word
``quantization.''
